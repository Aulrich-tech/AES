What AES is

AES — the Advanced Encryption Standard — is the symmetric block cipher NIST selected to replace DES and 3DES. The algorithm underneath it is called Rijndael, named for its two Belgian designers, Vincent Rijmen and Joan Daemen. It's symmetric, meaning the same key encrypts and decrypts, and it's a block cipher, meaning it always works on fixed 16-byte (128-bit) chunks of data regardless of the key size chosen.

Rijndael supports three key sizes — 128, 192, and 256 bits — and the key size decides how many rounds the cipher runs: 10 rounds for a 128-bit key, 12 for 192-bit, 14 for 256-bit. Every module in this reviewer uses the 128-bit / 10-round case, since that's what the worked examples are built on.

Where it's actually used

AES isn't a classroom-only cipher — it's the default symmetric cipher behind most of the encrypted systems already running around you:

  • Data and disk encryption — full-disk and file-level encryption on laptops and phones
  • TLS and IPSec — the encryption layer behind HTTPS and VPN tunnels
  • WPA2 — the encryption used to secure home and office Wi-Fi
  • Database encryption — protecting sensitive columns at rest
  • Messaging and cloud apps — securing data in mobile apps and cloud services
  • DRM and VPNs — protecting licensed content and private tunnels

How the two modules connect

Key Expansion and the Encryption Process aren't separate ciphers — they're two halves of the same pipeline. Key Expansion happens first and only once: it takes the original 16-byte key and stretches it into eleven 16-byte round keys (one for the initial AddRoundKey, and one per round). The Encryption Process then runs ten rounds over the plaintext, and every single one of those rounds consumes exactly one of the round keys that Key Expansion produced.
